Stories transport us. A role-playing game transports us as a participant.

A language model can now be the narrator.

Transportation

One breath. Narrative transportation is the psychologists' name for what a good story does: the reader leaves the room. A tabletop game does the same thing with the reader inside the story as a participant, deciding what happens next. And a language model can now take the narrator's chair. The next slide is the academic footing for that claim.

'Narrative Transportation'

  1. Stories change beliefs by transporting the reader — attention, imagery and feeling converge on the story and the room drops away.
  2. Narrative Transportation is a psychological measure of this phenomenon - it is measured with a 15-item questionnaire. Green & Brock 2000
  3. What raises it reads like a description of a role-playing game: interactivity, user control, perceived contingency, perspective-taking, imagination, mental simulation, spatial presence. Thomas & Grigsby 2024, Table 2
  4. The field has predicted this medium and not measured it — co-created stories where "the consumer will be the character", via "story prompts provided to AI interfaces". No LLM study among the 95 reviewed. Thomas & Grigsby 2024, §5.3
  5. What has been measured is prompt-only generation, and the evidence is thin. AI short stories transported readers slightly less than student-written ones (d = 0.21); a plain ChatGPT dungeon master scored 2.67/5 on immersion from three expert judges, through a translator; a GM with a memory agent and world state beat a prompt-only GM on immersion (N = 12). Appel et al. 2025; Triyason 2023; Jørgensen et al. 2025

The academic footing

Four points, one breath each. Transportation is a validated construct with a scale. The things that raise it are the things a game does by construction. The literature predicted this medium in its own future-research section and has not measured it. And what has been measured is the prompt-only version, with small samples: a small effect against AI-written stories on GPT-3.5, a low immersion score for a bare ChatGPT DM that the author himself says the translation setup depressed, and one within-subjects study where adding a memory agent and world state lifted immersion. Do not oversell it — the honest point is that nobody has measured a GM with a real world model, which is what this talk is about.

Mythras: a complete, mature, realistic, and risky RPG system.

Mythras

Say the four adjectives and move on. The next slide is what they mean.

What Mythras is

  • Mature. A system from The Design Mechanism (Nash and Whitaker), published as RuneQuest 6 in 2012 and as Mythras since 2016 — with a lineage that originates with the original RuneQuest of 1978.
  • Complete across genres. One core engine runs fantasy, semi-historical worlds (Mythic Babylon, Mythic Rome, Mythic Britain in the Dark Ages, Mythic Constantinople in the fifteenth century) — Jack Vance's Lyonesse, Bryan Talbot's Luther Arkwright, superheroes (Destined), science fiction (M-Space), with community-contributed genres extending the universes of possible play.
  • Realistic. Well-developed mechanisms: skills as percentages, opposed and differential rolls, action points, combat styles, hit locations with armor per location, special effects on a won exchange, fatigue, passions that push a roll, several frameworks for magic, superpowers, etc.
  • Risky. Combat is dangerous. A single won exchange can cripple or kill outright. It is easy to die in Mythras.

The Design Mechanism · Mythras Imperative and Classic Fantasy Imperative are free under the ORC licence.

What Mythras is

One line per adjective. Mature: this is the RuneQuest lineage, forty-odd years of d100 play, and the current rules are a decade old and stable. Complete: the same engine covers Babylon, Rome, Dark Ages Britain, fifteenth-century Constantinople, Vance's Lyonesse, superheroes, science fiction — which matters here because a world model that only works for one genre is a toy. Realistic: the mechanics are granular enough that the engine can adjudicate rather than the narrator. Risky: people die. That is not a bug; it is why the dice matter and why a GM who cannot arrange an outcome is a feature.

How things happen in Mythras

  • Named 'skills' manage capabilities: combat, brawn, stealth, perception, influence, deceit, acrobatics, etc..
  • A simple action requires a d100 roll under your skill's score.
  • A contested action is two rolls. Both sides roll against their own skill and the higher roll that still succeeded wins.
  • Game mechanics exist to permit the Gamesmaster (GM) to adjudicate most siutations.
  • The point. Every situation is resolved faithfully by the dice. It is a probabilistic model built from a small number of simple rules, whose outcomes compound — and nobody, the GM included, gets to choose them.

How things happen

Keep this at the level of a player, not an implementer. Seven characteristics; everything derives. One roll for a simple action, difficulty scales the target. Criticals are amazing, fumbles are catastrophic, and both happen to everyone. Two rolls for a contest, better grade wins — walk the example on the slide, all three branches. Combat is contests chained together, with the margin buying effects and the damage landing somewhere specific. Then the line: a probabilistic model from simple rules whose outcomes compound and that nobody chooses. That is what makes the next section — a world model — necessary rather than decorative.

A Gamesmaster manifests a world model for players.

The thesis

If the model is not written down, the voice will invent one — a different one each time.

The Mythras-GM Skill Models an RPG as Three Layers

  1. Rules. The Mythras Imperative and Classic Fantasy Imperative mechanics, as code and as a queryable graph: skill checks, opposed rolls, combat, magic, character generation. Outcomes are decided by dice, so play is intrinsically stochastic — the GM cannot arrange a result, and neither can the player.
  2. A living world. Every significant character and faction holds an agenda on a progress clock. Agendas produce beats — concrete things that will happen if nobody interferes — scheduled against a world clock and staged onscreen or offscreen by where the players actually are.
  3. Epistemics. Situational truth lives in one fact graph. Each character's knowledge is a projection of it through edges carrying certainty, source and time, so nobody can know what they were not there for, and a wrong belief is data rather than improvisation.

Every action, encounter, conversation and item lives in a TypeDB Database behind an 88-command CLI. The game is driven from that state, not from the transcript.

Three layers

This is the map for the next ten minutes. Rules are the easy layer and mostly code. The living world and the epistemics are the two that needed an ontology, and each came from a failure at the table.

Four questions

These four rows are the design document for the storage layer, and each was paid for with a real failure. The retired-crew one has a precise cause: the context loader filtered player characters to "active", so the old crew vanished from the save while their events stayed in the log with no visible owner. Orphaned history attaches itself to whoever is on screen.

Mythras-GM: an agent with an ontological notebook memory

  • Agent. Claude Code, run interactively. You talk; the agent curates. It was built to read scientific papers, disease databases and job postings and build a structured understanding that persists across sessions.
  • TypeDB. Ontological memory. The schema defines the concepts the agent reasons about; the data is what it has learned so far. Entities, relations and attributes are typed, and a query is a pattern over that type system.
  • Skills. A skill is not a prompt. It contributes a schema namespace, a CLI for reading and writing that namespace, and the agent's instructions for the domain. Each one extends what the agent can do and remember.
  • Gaps. When the agent tries to represent something the schema has no place for, that gap is the primary signal for evolving the graph. Gaps are filed and triaged like bugs.

Alhazen

The gamesmaster is one skill in this operating system. Its namespace is myth-. Its concepts are on the next slide. The same discipline that keeps a literature notebook honest — everything typed, everything attributed — is what a game needed.

The myth- namespace: a world that moves and is known

The ontology

Walk it left to right. An agenda is a goal held by a named person or faction, on a progress clock. It schedules beats: the concrete things it will produce if nobody interferes, placed in world time with a location and a cast. A beat that fires becomes a journal event, and events make facts true.

Then the two edges that make it a world model rather than a timeline: knows, from character to fact, carrying certainty, source and since; and the feedback arrows — a fact can advance, thwart or activate an agenda, and an agenda can require a fact, so "the Baron acts the moment he sees that face" is computed, not remembered.

A believed falsehood is a first-class object

  • A fact carries status (not-yet-true → established) and truth independently. A rumour, a lie and a sincere misreading are all data, not GM improvisation.
  • Perspective lives on the edge, not in the world. Two characters can hold the same event and read it incompatibly; neither reading changes the journal.
  • A debt is a belief plus a feeling. There is no ledger of obligations — the villain who feels slighted feels owed revenge, and that lives in the attitude on his edge.
  • Second-order knowledge needs no machinery. "Nobody knows he knows" is one edge with one knower.
  • check-consistency catches a character knowing a thing before it was true — the error that otherwise hides in prose until it contradicts play.

Status times truth

The trick is separating two axes a transcript conflates: whether a proposition has happened yet, and whether it is true. Once those are independent, a false belief is not a hack — it is an edge with a wrong reading on it.

Living world

This is the fix for "it freezes".

The mechanics wall, as architecture

Architecture

The dashed line is the design principle made physical. Left of it, prose. Right of it, JSON. A campaign is a plugin: a folder of markdown and JSON in git that imports into the database with one command and exports back losslessly. The novelist reads the journal and never writes back.

tick: staging is read from presence, not chosen

  • Between scenes the GM advances the world clock. Every beat now due is reported onscreen (a PC is at its location or in its cast — play it) or offscreen (it happens anyway and becomes a fact they may later discover).
  • Move the party and the attack they would have interrupted becomes the one they hear about at dawn. The GM cannot rail, because staging is derived, not picked.
  • Offscreen beats roll dice too. An NPC's plan can fail in a room nobody is in, and the world is different in the morning because of it. Players learn of it only through consequences — no cutaways.
  • Clocks advance when the fiction earns it; agendas can be thwarted; a stale beat is revised rather than held to. It is structure, not a rail.

Steer the world. Never the character.

The line

This resolves the apparent contradiction between "be a storyteller with an agenda" and "never railroad".

How to run the table

  1. Show it. Do not explain it. A fact the player could find is worth ten a character tells them. Information lives in objects: a ledger, a scar, a missing chair, a wet bootprint.
  2. The player's mind is not yours. No thoughts, no conclusions, no decisions. Describe the world; the player supplies the mind.
  3. Never hand out a read. What an NPC feels is what an Insight roll buys. An NPC saying "she was afraid" is an unearned roll wearing a costume.
  4. Stop where the decision is. Play the turn until the player has something to decide, then hand over plainly. Never a menu of options, never a named skill.
  • Three doors, never one. A scene the story needs should be reachable three ways. A beat that requires the party in one room at one hour is a badly built beat; fix the beat, not the players.
  • If they will not come to the scene, the scene comes to them. Same content, different delivery.
  • A hook not taken comes back changed. Louder, costlier, or out of a different mouth.
  • Missing something is never less game. Whoever is not at the crime gets the crime scene at dawn.
  • Every attempt gets dice. A failed roll narrows options; it never ends the scene.

The table

The conduct file is about a thousand lines and the engineering notes say it is the most important document in the repo. Left column: what the GM may never do to the character. Right column: what the GM should always be doing to the world.

Where the rules came from

The player, mid-session, verbatim

Ok. Stop.

You're not playing this like a Mythras game. You're narrating.

You should be making rolls every time we try to do something. I don't immediately see that Emmerelda's scared. That takes an insight roll. I don't immediately lose him, I should have a chance of catching him.

The descriptive passages you're generating are too long and putting words into my mouth for the sake of the story.

This needs to be interactive. With a lot more open ended play

Banned constructions — measured tics, not style preferences. Every rule is countable, because "be less verbose" has never once worked.

  • "arithmetic" — banned as a word, not just a metaphor
  • "That's not X. It's Y." — the antithesis correction, zero per scene
  • "Two things." — the enumerated preamble: 25 times in two sessions, in five characters' mouths. Changing the noun does not change the habit, so the rule bans the move.
  • The withheld ending — "and past that point I —" — once per campaign
  • "It cost her something to say it" — that is a read
  • Any header, rule or bulleted recap inside table prose

The correction

The actual message that produced the conduct file. Every complaint maps to a rule: rolls for every attempt, no free reads, failure narrows rather than ends, no interior for the player's character, short beats, open-ended. The banned list is the interesting engineering — a record of failures kept in the shape a model can obey.

Live play

Real transcript, same session, after the correction. Every attempt gets a roll, shown in one line and never mentioned in prose. "Am I hidden?" gets an honest answer about what the roll bought. The player withdraws and is not punished or nagged. Then the villain's plan runs offscreen, on dice — he succeeds at the thing he came for and fumbles the escape — and the player hears a house go wrong from three floors up. The splash under the window is now a fact they can go and find.

Write it the way it was played.

The rule

Because the journal is structured, it can be read back out as a manuscript. But summaries are not dialogue.

The journal becomes a book

  • Extract. The journal and player-visible canon come out of TypeDB as a source document. Canon flows one way; nothing written here goes back.
  • Outline by story turns, not sessions. A chapter is one movement — a stretch with one question in it. A session boundary is an accident of somebody's evening and means nothing to a reader.
  • Pin a style card. Hemingway, Tolkien, Moorcock, or the house voice derived from the author's own draft — copied into the manuscript so the book is reproducible in a later session.
  • Draft from the transcript. Journal events are summaries; they carry what happened and none of what was said. The first draft of chapter one invented a confrontation where the real scene was a friendly conversation, put four men on a bridge where there were two, and lost every real line. Now the player's own words go on the page as close to verbatim as prose will carry.
  • Typeset. pandoc and Typst render the PDF.

The economic argument.

A campaign is a folder

  • A scripted game authors every branch it will ever allow. A world model authors the situation — who wants what, on what clock — and the model generates whatever the player actually does.
  • Purewater is a few evenings of writing. It has been played four different ways, by four different characters, and each run diverged from the plan on the first day.
  • When play leaves the plan the answer is a rewrite, not a loading screen: revise the downstream beats, rewrite the act summaries, keep going. The catalog must describe the game being played, not the one that was planned.
  • No art pipeline, no level design, no dialogue trees. An 88-command CLI and a rules graph are the whole runtime.
  • Publishing is a git push. Anyone can load it with one command; export is lossless, so a played campaign can be published as a new seed.

Superhuman gamesmastering.

Superhuman

In the plain sense: bookkeeping no human GM has ever been able to do.

What no human gamesmaster can do — and what still needs one

  • Hold 42 characters' knowledge with per-fact provenance — certainty, source, since — and never leak.
  • Run fifteen agendas off-camera, on dice, without contradicting itself, for as many sessions as the game lasts.
  • Run a consistency check that catches a character learning a thing before it was true.
  • Roll every roll, honestly, including against itself — and show the number in one line, then never mention it.
  • Answer a rules question by query over 271 faceted rule nodes rather than by memory.
  • Typeset the novel by morning, in Moorcock's voice if you like.
  • The three layers — rules, agendas on clocks, projected knowledge — are a world model for any domain where things happen off-camera and different people know different things.
  • Taste. The style card is derived from the author's own draft.
  • Conduct. The table rules are a record of corrections, each one paid for at the table by a real player who stopped the game to say so.
  • The scenario. Where the seam in Alderic Vantt is was written by a person with the whole arc in view.
  • The evening. The model is the voice; the ontology is the world; the human is the one who knows what a good evening feels like. The system is a way for that taste to be enforced at scale, not replaced.
