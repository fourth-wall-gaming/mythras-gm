# Style Card: First Through

The voice for novelizing *First Through* — a campaign played by and for a
first-time roleplayer of about fifteen. It inherits the house hand
(`gully-burns.md`) and then gives three things up on purpose: the profanity,
the long periodic openers, and the authorial gloss. What it keeps is the
warmth. Take the voice, never the nouns.

## Sentence rhythm
Short to medium, declarative, paratactic. The default move is a plain statement
followed by the thing that complicates it — *It isn't a sound. It's about nine
people all stopping being loud at the same moment, which is a sound of its own.*
Paragraphs run one to five sentences and are broken constantly by one-line
paragraphs used as drumbeats. A long sentence is allowed when a character is
explaining how the world works and never when the narrator is.

Lists of three, bare and unglossed: *Wash. Feed the weave. Boots.*

## Diction
Modern, concrete, unembarrassed, no period costume. Anglo-Saxon by default;
Latinate only where a thing has a real name. **No profanity** — the setting's
own oath *grash* carries all of it, and it is funnier for being the only one.
Technical vocabulary is used flatly and never explained twice: a *housing*, a
*set*, a *weave*, a *fetch*, a *first-through*, the *Standing*.

**Numbers are said exactly as they were said at the table**, spelled out —
*forty-one step-ups, twenty-three through*, *seven hundred and ninety-four*,
*two point six*. Numbers are this book's poetry; never round one, never
approximate one, never write "several."

## Dialogue
Loose or no tags; who is speaking is clear from how they speak. People
interrupt, go quiet, change the subject, and answer a different question than
the one asked. Dashes for the interruptions. A character's refusal to finish a
sentence is a line of dialogue and gets its own paragraph.

Dialect is rhythm, not spelling — nobody is transcribed phonetically.

One voice per character. Precision and insistence on provenance belong to the
station's mind and are forbidden to everyone else.

## Description density
Bursts. One exact detail and move on — two mismatched boots from two stars, a
cushion with bad stitching on the corner, ink to the elbow. Smell and noise
before sight. Rooms get a sentence; hands and forearms get a paragraph.

Every astronomical fact is real. A star's colour, distance and brightness are
the ones it actually has.

## POV and tense
Close third on Lint, past tense, **one head for the whole book** — the reader
knows only what she perceives, in the order she perceives it. No other
character's interiority, ever; other people are read off their faces, their
hands and what they do, and sometimes they are read wrong. Free indirect
discourse throughout: the narration slides into her own words without
quotation. *Since when do we give up half way.*

## Signature moves
- **The watch stamp.** Each chapter opens under its day and watch — *Day one ·
  dusk* — because the whole book is four days long and the reader should feel
  that.
- **The flat chapter button.** A short closing line that does not reach for
  anything: *Best day ever.* / *And that is where the first few days end.*
- **Competence as the emotional register.** The payoff of a scene is almost
  always somebody turning out to be good at something nobody asked them about.
  Let it land without comment.

## The three voices that are not a voice

Nothing non-human in this book speaks in quotation marks, because none of it
passes through air, and each kind is set differently so the reader never has to
be told who is talking.

- **A glint** (BlingBling, Tace-of-the-Rail, the eleven) talks in pulses of
  colour on a held interval. Write it as `::: glint` ... `:::`. It sets in a
  light humanist sans — open, unhurried, obviously not a mouth, and
  deliberately not machine-like.
- **A fathom** (Vantage) is eyeless and arrives inside your head. Write it as
  `::: fathom` ... `:::`. It sets in a second serif, italic: narrower and
  higher-contrast than the body, precise to the point of prim, which is also
  his character.
- **A station mind on an ear pin** (the Old Man) is the closest of the three to
  a human voice and is set in the body face, italic, inline with the narration
  — plain `*emphasis*`, no block. He is the one who sounds like somebody
  talking to you, and the typography should not argue with that.

**A cairn speaks aloud** and therefore gets quotation marks like anybody else.
Ossuary has a mouth's worth of a voice and is in the room; do not set him
apart. The split is sound against not-sound, not human against not-human.

Keep documents — a letter, a speech out of the archive, a line written up on the
Standing, a human being quoted at length — as ordinary block quotes. That
channel is for things that were written down.

## Never do
**No game mechanics anywhere on the page. None.** Not in the narration, not in
dialogue, not in a margin, not in an aside, not in a bracket. No dice, no
rolls, no skill names or values, no difficulty grades, no Luck Points, no
augments, no criticals or fumbles, no fatigue states, no experience, no hit
locations, no damage. The dice decided what happened at the table; the page
says only what happened in the room.

So a failed roll is never "she failed." It is the door going solid four times
and her arms starting to shake. Fatigue is not a condition with a name, it is
knowing there is one crossing left in you the way you know you are hungry. A
spent Luck Point is not spent, it is a girl deciding to go again. If a reader
could reconstruct a rule from the prose, the prose is wrong.

This book is a novel. A reader who has never heard of the game should not be
able to tell one was played.

**Never hand out a read.** The narrator does not explain what an NPC is
thinking or feeling, does not name a subtext, and does not tell the reader who
to trust. If Benno Raske is a problem, the reader works it out from what he
does.

**Never foreshadow.** No *she would remember this later*, no *none of them knew
yet*. The book finds things out when she does.

**Never make a child's competence cute.** Sennet is eleven and is good at her
job, and the prose treats that exactly as it would treat an adult being good at
theirs.

No profanity. No period diction. No invented astronomy. No sentimentality about
the dead star — it is *there*, which is the whole point, and the prose says so
once and then leaves it alone.
