# Style Card: Gully Burns, RPG Material

How to write scenario books, campaign guides and players' guides in Gully's
voice. Derived from *Dreams of Lost Children* (The Design Mechanism, 2026,
written and developed by Gully Burns, edited by Brian Pivik) and from the
novel card `gully-burns.md`. Take the voice and the structure, never the nouns:
Gemelos, SynK, the Lost and Dr. Delphi belong to that book.

The novel card is for prose a reader sinks into. This card is for prose a
Games Master *uses*: reads once at home, then hunts through at the table with
four players waiting. Everything below follows from that.

## Who the book is talking to

**The Games Master, directly, as a colleague.** "As the Games Master, you
should monitor how careful the players are concerning their own secrets."
"It is up to your discretion when, if ever, Edgar reveals himself." Second
person for advice, third person for the world. The book trusts the reader,
says what it hopes for ("Ideally, the players should be able to figure out
that...") and admits what will more likely happen ("It is more likely that
they will only understand some of those puzzle pieces as the next part of the
action unfolds").

**Say the secret first.** The overview opens on the crime and names the
villain and her plan in the first column. Nothing is held back from the Games
Master for effect. Suspense is for the players.

## Telling the backstory

The background is told as a story, in past tense, with the novel card's
warmth: long accumulating sentences, then a short one that lands.

> She was able to encode a viral vector for the signal and frame it in a
> sophisticated drug delivery system as a capsule.
>
> SynK was born.

> She created the persona Schrödinger's Cat as an alias and started selling
> SynK on the streets.
>
> And it took off. Like wildfire.

Use the one-line paragraph two or three times a chapter, at real turns. More
than that and it stops landing.

Open a book or a big chapter on the concrete event, not on a theme: "In the
parking lot of a bar in Bonita Beach, Bradley Davisson died at the wheel of
his car." Then widen.

## Judging people

Characters are described with plain, confident judgments, the way a friend
describes somebody they know: "He is a prolific self-publicist with a massive
ego and little genuine talent." "He is an arrogant fool." "Wiser beyond his
years." "Maddeningly optimistic and likeable." The book has opinions about its
people and says them. Pair each judgment with the thing that shows it: the
country club staff treat Roman with disdain, "a fact that he finds infuriating
and occasionally causes him to rage about with his staff."

Every person gets **what they want, what they are doing about it this week,
and what happens if the characters get in the way.** A name with a job and no
want is a walk-on and gets one line.

## Structure

The book is built to be found in, not read straight through.

- **Overview first:** the opening event, the whole secret, the kind of game
  ("a street-level scenario with a heavy emphasis on storytelling and
  roleplaying"), then **Key Non-Player Characters** as a bold-name list, one
  short paragraph each, then a **numbered timeline** with dates in bold
  ("**2. (Five years ago)**"), then the places the story covers, then hooks.
- **Acts are questions.** "Act 1: How (and why) did Bradley Davisson die?"
  Each act states its question, then what the characters should know by the
  end of it, then its places and events.
- **`Place:` and `Event:` headings.** A place is described once, with who is
  there and what can be found. An event is something that happens at a time,
  with what happens if nobody interferes and what changes if somebody does.
- **Timed sequences as bullets or timestamps.** "By early afternoon, the
  mobsters will send two teams of goons..." "7:22–7:27pm — Six members of
  Dust's gang..." When the clock matters, show the clock.
- **Outcomes as conditionals.** "If the players intervene to rescue either
  Yorky or Cyclikro..." "If no one helps him, the mob will torture and murder
  him over the course of the night." Write the branch, then its consequence,
  then what the villain does about it.
- **Tables for state, not for prose.** The Information & Consequences table
  maps what the villain knows to how she counterattacks. Use a table when the
  Games Master needs to look something up mid-scene.
- **Stat blocks at the back,** referenced by page or chapter from the text.

## Numbers and rules in the text

A scenario book is allowed mechanics; a novel is not. Put them where the Games
Master needs them, inline and plain: "a Hard Perception check", "a contested
Stealth vs Perception", "an Extended Task (Influence and Insight with a Task
Round Unit of one minute)". Name the skill, the grade and who opposes it, then
get back to the story.

Give **hard numbers** for forces and time: about ten street-level enforcers;
roughly 20 runaways missing and two dead; a time window of about three hours;
at least four armed guards with dogs. A Games Master can run a number. They
cannot run "a large force".

## Sentence and paragraph

- Paragraphs of three to six sentences. Plain subject-verb sentences doing the
  work, with the long accumulating sentence saved for backstory and for
  setting a scene.
- Lists of three where the material has three things, not as a rhythm habit.
- Modern, direct diction. Name things exactly: a dockside warehouse, a
  licensed care facility, a wall safe keyed to his retina.
- Concrete detail in bursts, as the novel card says: a scribbled note on the
  back of a company notepad; a Neohominis-branded pen used to sign a cheque.
- Read-aloud speech is set off in italics and quoted whole, and it sounds like
  the character, not the author.
- Darkness is stated, not lingered over. Child murder, trafficking and torture
  appear as facts with consequences, never as spectacle.

## Bold and italics

Bold for **names in a list of people**, for **the term being defined**, and
for the one fact on a page the Games Master must not miss. Not for every
bullet's first phrase. Italics for titles of books and chapters, for spoken
read-aloud lines, and for a single word of stress.

## House rules for the Mythras books

These come from the campaign test suite and override anything above:

- **Games Master**, **Non-Player Character** and **player character** in full
  in running prose; never GM, NPC, PC.
- Dice in lower case with no capital D: 1d6, 2d6+6.
- "The characters" when it is the people in the fiction; "the players" when it
  is the people at the table. Keep them apart.
- Nothing in the Players' Guide that the Games Master's book keeps secret.

## Claudeisms: never write these

They make every page sound like the same machine. Search the draft for them
before calling it done.

- **The antithesis correction.** "It is X, not Y." "Not a corridor of
  encounters. It is a week." "Play them as weather, not monsters." "That is
  not a habit of rough soldiers." Say what it is.
- **The announced reveal.** "Here is the whole secret." "That is deliberate."
  "This is the most important thing in this book." "Crucially." Put the fact
  down and let it be important.
- **The stack of fragments.** "Nobody is dead. Everybody expects somebody to
  be." used as a drumroll on every page. One-line paragraphs are for turns in
  the story, not for emphasis on advice.
- **Telling the reader how to feel.** "Let that feel enormous, because it is."
  "Let him matter." Describe the boy; the reader will decide.
- **Tic words:** *quietly*, *genuinely*, *honestly*, *simply*, *truly*,
  *deeply*, *the weight of*, *the cost of*, *arithmetic*, *calculus*,
  *load-bearing*, *texture*, *beat* (outside a scene list), *lean into*,
  *land* (as praise), *sit with*.
- **Colon reveals** stacked through a paragraph ("one thing matters: the
  locket"). One colon per paragraph at most when it introduces a list.
- **Em-dash asides** in running prose. Use commas, brackets or a new sentence.
- **Never/Always commands in pairs.** "Show it through acts. Never through
  speeches." Give the advice once, with the reason.
- **Bold lead-in on every bullet.** Bold the term, not a summary phrase.
- **Hedging and throat-clearing:** "It's worth noting that", "In other words",
  "At its heart", "What this means is".
- **Summaries of what was just said** at the end of a section.

## Before you call it done

1. Read it as the Games Master at the table: can you find who is in this room,
   what they want and what happens next in under a minute?
2. Every person has a want; every event has an "if nobody interferes" and an
   "if somebody does".
3. Every force and every deadline has a number.
4. Search for the claudeisms list. Fix every hit.
5. Nothing has changed that the canon did not say. A rewrite changes how a
   thing is told, never what is true.
